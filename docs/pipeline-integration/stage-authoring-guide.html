A stage is a contract.
A conversation is not.

The numbers in the criteria — a pass at 12 of 16, three criteria that must be at 2, the agent's 8 of 10 — are this page's proposal and are marked †. The failures the criteria are written against are not: each one is attributed to the SUG id that found it.

01The test a stage has to pass

Given this input, produce that artifact, checked this way. A stage that cannot be said that way is a conversation, and conversations do not deploy.

Source: docs/pipeline/stages.md — opening; README.md §1; agents.md — opening and §9

The pipeline's own definition is three clauses: one input artifact, one producing role, one machine-checkable exit. Thirteen stages are written to it, S0 to S12. That sentence is the whole test, and it is harder to satisfy than it looks: the corpus is explicit that the exit check and the gate are different objects, that an agent may not choose the next stage, and that a rule with no check is advice.

The four questions, and what an unanswered one costs

The distinction is not cosmetic. Twenty-two agents, two services and one job are on the roster, and the two services are exactly the places where a model's judgement would make the evidence unusable: the freeze and the verifier. An orchestrator agent is on the list of roles that must not exist, because if a model picks the next stage, "why did it deploy?" has no deterministic answer.

Two claims keep the chain honest

One writer per artifact — freeze job, teardown crews, rebuild agent, reviewer panel, fixer, verifier, delivery plane, never two — and the ledger is append-only, including for refusals. The twelve fields in §02 are the smallest set that lets a control plane enforce both without reading a single sentence of prose.

The sentence that decides whether a field is needed

work-breakdown.md §5 states it for the packet, and it holds for the stage: "A packet is the agent's whole world for one run. If a field is missing, the agent will invent it." An invented input, exit or boundary is not a gap in a document; it is work performed against a different contract than the one that was reviewed.

02The twelve-field contract

A stage that cannot fill all twelve has not been designed. It has been described.

Sources: stages.md §S0–§S12; agents.md §1, §9, §10, §11; rules.md §1, §12; work-breakdown.md §5

Each field below is stated three ways: what it fixes, the shape a value must take, and what a missing value causes. The shape is deliberately mechanical — a control plane reads it, and a reviewer checks it without re-reading the stage's prose. The sources column names where the field's requirement comes from, so a reader can tell a rule in the corpus from this page's formulation of it.

Twelve fields. Columns 2 and 4 are the reason the field exists: what it fixes, and what its absence breaks.

Field by field

F01id

The stage's permanent number. A new stage takes the next number; a renamed stage keeps its own. Nothing in this directory renumbers, which is why two proposals that would have renumbered the spine — splitting S9 into S9a/S9b and adding a fourteenth teardown stage — were reduced to separate caps and a G5 precondition instead.

id: S7 # S0..S12 exist; the next new stage is S13 or none

Source: rules.md §1 on permanent ids, applied to stages; SUG-GATE-09, SUG-GATE-14.

Refuse: an id already in the ledger, or a stage addressed only by its title.

F02name

The act, not the artifact: "Freeze and export", not "The export"; "DAL review and SQL verify", not "DAL quality". An "and" is allowed when one exit check covers both halves — S2's ten-line checklist and S7's DAL verdict each do — and forbidden when each half has its own exit, because then it is two stages and one of them has no owner.

name: DAL review and SQL verify # verb phrase; the same string in the table and the ledger

Source: stages.md §S2, §S7; SUG-GATE-09 on two exits in one entry.

Refuse: a noun phrase, or a conjunction whose halves have different exit checks.

F03purpose

One sentence, and it has to name the artifact as well as the act, because the artifact is what the next stage is allowed to read. "Produce the semantic layer from the frozen export, so the rebuild has a contract it must match" is a purpose; "understand the POC" is a wish. Two sentences mean two stages, or one stage with an unstated second exit.

purpose: >- Turn the frozen export into the six layer files, so the rebuild has a contract it must match and G2 has something to validate.

Source: stages.md §S3 "Takes / Does / Produces"; README.md §1.

Refuse: a purpose with no artifact in it, or a second sentence.

F04owner

Exactly one role, and its kind is part of the field. An agent has judgement inside its remit and no say in what happens next; a service or job has neither; a human seat has both, at a gate, with accountability. The control plane owns the order — a stage whose owner picks the next stage is not a stage in this pipeline.

owner: {role: dal-reviewer, kind: agent} owner: {role: verifier, kind: service} # deterministic; no discretion

Source: agents.md opening table, §1 roster, §12 "roles that must not exist".

Refuse: two owners, an unnamed owner, or an owner that also owns the stage's check.

F05inputs

An enumerated list, one entry per artifact, and every entry carries a hash and a location. A branch path is not a location: the same packet run tomorrow reads different code. The corpus has nine roles with no Reads field at all, and the verifier is the one that matters most — an input nobody names cannot be granted, denied, hashed or audited. The list is also a non-list: what the stage may not read is part of the field, because the verifier reading the fixer's transcript would anchor it to the author's account of the change.

inputs: read: - {artifact: layer, schema: layer/1, location: "ledger#L-0042", sha256: "9f2c…", mutability: read-only} - {artifact: finding-set, schema: finding/2, location: "ledger#F-0018", sha256: "a71b…", mutability: read-only} non: [fixer-transcript, reviewer-confidence]

Source: SUG-VER-01, SUG-ROLE-38, SUG-GATE-03; P-69, P-71.

Refuse: a path without a hash; an input named in prose; no non-input list where a neighbouring role's reasoning exists.

F06trigger

Two parts: who fires the stage, and the condition that must hold. "The control plane, when the producer's envelope carries a status of OK and every input hash resolves" is a trigger. "After S6" is not, because it does not say what makes S6's output usable. A new push re-fires the stage and invalidates the previous approval, which is the same rule the verifier applies to its own verdict.

trigger: fires: control-plane when: "envelope.status == OK and all(inputs[].sha256) resolve" # else REFUSE-INPUT

Source: agents.md §9 stop vocabulary; P-53; SUG-VER-11.

Refuse: a trigger expressed as a position in a list rather than a predicate.

F07binds

The rule ids that constrain the work, in three namespaces that must not be mixed: P-* for the pipeline's own rules, SEFL-* for the standards pack, and R-* only through a rule's Provenance field. Each id names the check that enforces it, because the corpus's own test is that a rule with no check is advice. A PROPOSED rule may run in a pilot but may not block a merge, which is why a stage that binds one has to say what it does when that rule is the only failing instrument.

binds: - {id: P-52, check: "verifier signature verifies and subject.commit == HEAD"} - {id: P-41, check: "instrument manifest hash equals the pinned hash"} - {id: SEFL-DATA-004, check: "every statement has a verification id"} # domain slice, see §08 - {id: P-42, tag: PROPOSED, on_fail: "record regime; cannot block until adopted"}

Source: rules.md §1 tag table and §12; SUG-VER-10; SUG-GATE-04.

Refuse: "the standards pack" as a value; an id with no check; a bare R-number.

F08method

What the owner does, in order, and whether judgement is involved. The corpus splits the two: mechanical facts are extracted by code, meaning by a model, and every model reading must quote the line it judged. Where the method hits an ambiguity it produces a question — both readings, one record — rather than a decision, because ambiguity that gets resolved silently is the failure the layer's citation rule exists to prevent.

method: deterministic: [resolve-named-checks, hash-subject, run-suite-twice, run-holdouts] judgement: [] # a service; no model in the path on_ambiguity: ESCALATE-AMBIGUITY # both readings, one record

Source: P-06, P-10, P-13; stages.md §S9 table of proved steps.

Refuse: a method that names tools but not order, or a model step with no citation rule.

F09artifact

One artifact, with a schema, a location, a hash and a single writer. "Tier-1 results", "install SUCCESS" and a prose report are not artifacts: a free-text result cannot be joined to a rule id, a finding, or a gate, and the pipeline's blocking power becomes whatever a reader makes of prose. The schema at the boundary is the one measured fix for handoff fidelity, and the artifact system — a specialist writes to a store and passes a reference back — is what stops a long output being copied through a conversation.

artifact: id: verdict path: "ledger/verdicts/V-0001.json" schema: "verdict/1" sha256: "<computed by the signer>" writer: verifier-signer # exactly one; append-only

Source: SUG-ROLE-22, SUG-ROLE-32, SUG-ROLE-20, SUG-ROLE-35; README.md §1.

Refuse: two writers, no schema, or an artifact identified only by a directory.

F10done

The machine-checkable exit, one record per line: an id, a command, a pass condition and the evidence the command leaves. Every line must be a command or a set operation. The corpus's own gate rule decides the tone — a blank line is a refusal, not a pass — and S2 shows the shape a good exit takes: three conjuncts, not one, because a single export hash proves an export happened, not that it is reproducible.

done: - {check_id: EXIT-01, command: "sha256sum -c export.sha256", pass_when: "exit 0"} - {check_id: EXIT-02, command: "diff <(jq . a.json) <(jq . b.json)", pass_when: "empty diff"} - {check_id: EXIT-03, command: "jq -e '.subject.commit == env.HEAD' verdict.json", pass_when: "true"}

Source: gates.md §2 "a blank line is a refusal"; SUG-GATE-01, SUG-ROLE-13.

Refuse: a line a reader has to evaluate ("reviewed", "looks consistent"); a check with no id.

F11stops

The stage's honest ways to say no, drawn from the seven-code vocabulary rather than invented per stage: REFUSE-INPUT, ESCALATE-AMBIGUITY, ESCALATE-NO-PROGRESS, REJECT-SCOPE, STOP-BUDGET, RECLASSIFY, HANDOFF-HUMAN. Each carries a destination and, for a loop, a cap. Two of the seven are success, not failure — a clean reclassification and a clean ambiguity escalation — and a stage with no state for "the check could not run" has a cheap path that reads it as green.

stops: - {code: REFUSE-INPUT, when: "named check does not resolve; hash mismatch", to: control-plane} - {code: ESCALATE-NO-PROGRESS, when: "two runs disagree", to: engineer, cap: flake-budget} - {code: REJECT-SCOPE, when: "assertion drift with no test-change record", to: control-plane}

Source: agents.md §9; P-50, P-51; SUG-VER-07.

Refuse: a free-text escape hatch, or a stop with no destination.

F12forbidden

The must-nots, each split into Enforced by — a grant, a frozen hash, a CI check, a deny rule — or Judged: what the agent weighs and what it does instead. A line with neither is deleted. This is the test rules.md §12 already applies to its own rules, and applying it to the roster is what separates a boundary from a preference. Two of the boundaries have to be mechanical rather than stated: the frozen manifest is closed under addition, and the blast radius is a set comparison.

forbidden: - {line: "edit the branch or the tests", enforced_by: "read-only clone; file:write deny rule"} - {line: "hold the signing key in the runner", enforced_by: "separate signer identity; KMS key"} - {line: "read the fixer's transcript", judged: "anchors the verdict; the non-input is the control"} - {line: "widen the blast radius", enforced_by: "patch file list ⊄ blast_radius.paths → REJECT-SCOPE"}

Source: SUG-ROLE-37, SUG-VER-04, SUG-ROLE-28; P-44, P-54, P-72.

Refuse: a must-not with neither an enforcement nor a judgement; a boundary an agent interprets.

03The stage rubric, 0–2 per criterion

Eight criteria, sixteen points, and three of them cannot be traded against the others.

Sources: stages.md §S0–§S12; gates.md §1–§2; SUG-GATE-01, 02, 04, 09, 10; SUG-VER-01, 02, 03, 07, 10, 13; SUG-ROLE-13, 20, 22, 32, 35, 38

The twelve fields are the contract; the rubric is the test of whether the contract is real. Each criterion is scored 0, 1 or 2 against what is written, not what the author intended, and each anchor is a sentence a second reader can disagree with — which is the point of a rubric. The criteria are not independent: a stage that scores 2 on the artifact and 0 on the exit check has an artifact nobody can accept or reject.

Eight criteria, 0–2. The anchors are deliberately concrete: a 2 is something a stranger can verify from the file.

Pass threshold †

A stage passes at 12 of 16, and only if C2, C3 and C5 each score 2. Everything below 12 is a conversation; a stage at 12 with a 1 on any of those three is a stage that cannot prove it finished, cannot hand anything over, or grades itself.

The three locked criteria are locked because each one has a documented failure behind it: prose exits are how a review round produces the same findings three times (S8), messages instead of artifacts are how "install SUCCESS" survived 46 of 176 bundles not being ready (I-4, SUG-ROLE-32), and a producer with a reachable instrument is how a green suite comes to mean nothing (SUG-VER-13). The number 12, and the choice of exactly these three, is this page's judgement — the corpus states the criteria, not the weights.

The trap in a scored rubric

A score is a finding, not a verdict on the team. The failure mode is the one the review chapters name: a panel that grades everything 2 because the author is in the room. The corpus's answer is the sampled audit — 10% of machine-approved artifacts read in full by someone who did not approve them (P-76, D9) — and the same discipline applies here: a rubric scored only by the author of the stage is a 16/16 that means nothing.

04The agent that works a stage

A role has one remit, one input, one output and one stop condition. Five criteria decide whether the role card says so.

Sources: agents.md §1, §9, §10, §11, §12; SUG-ROLE-18, 24, 35, 37, 38; SUG-VER-01, 07, 12

The stage contract says what the work is. The agent rubric says whether the worker can be trusted with it — not in the moral sense, in the mechanical one: does it have the capability the method needs, the context the artifact list allows, a list of things it may not do that something enforces, a self-check it must run, and one honest way to stop. Five criteria, two points each, and the same shape as the stage rubric so a reviewer can hold both in mind at once.

Five criteria, 0–2. A3 and A5 are the ones that turn a prompt into a boundary.

The envelope, because a self-check is not a report

Every role returns the same shape, so the control plane never parses prose. The four fields the corpus adds are the ones a self-check needs to be checkable: which version of the artifact, whether the run stopped, what it consumed, and what it ran.

Confidence is not authority: a 0.95 with no citation fails; a 0.6 with a line number and a named question passes to the reviewer. The number routes attention; it does not decide.

VerifierRead the branch; write signed resultsBe written to by any agent
AuditorRead everything in the sampleChoose the sample; write to the stage

A grant is per stage and per artifact. The rebuild agent necessarily touches user wording and can push, so its egress is one branch on one internal repository and its model calls go through the gateway — it is not trusted to behave; it is denied the ability to matter outside its branch.

Four roles the roster forbids

An orchestrator agent — if a model picks the next stage, "why did it deploy?" has no deterministic answer. A self-reviewing agent — the producer and the judge are different roles with different context, and the judge's instrument is out of the producer's reach. A general-purpose engineer agent holding repo, Oracle, Tower and promote credentials — maximum capability is maximum blast radius. An agent that dispositions its own findings — Sentinel and the reviewers raise, the adjudicator merges, the verifier closes.

05The definition in YAML, and the prompt it becomes

One file, twelve keys. The prompt is generated from the file; it is not written beside it.

Sources: field shapes from §02; packet fields in work-breakdown.md §5; envelope in agents.md §10; SUG-GATE-03, SUG-ROLE-18, SUG-ROLE-35

The definition is the artifact a reviewer reads and a control plane executes. The prompt is a projection of it, one slot per field, in the same order — which is what makes "the agent was given the packet" a checkable claim rather than a good intention. If a slot is empty in the definition, the prompt has a hole where an instruction would be, and the agent fills it with invention.

06Worked example — the verifier stage

The only actor that closes a finding, written out as a stage: twelve fields, one signed artifact, and a fourth answer for the case the documents cannot express.

Sources: agents.md §7 Verifier; stages.md §S9; suggestions/verifier-role.md SUG-VER-01..13; rules.md P-41–P-53; pipeline.html §11.1–§11.2

The verifier is chosen because it is the hardest case. Its output is the verdict of record, its subject is a commit it did not write, its instrument is code from the branch it is judging, and the corpus records that the documents give it no input list, no record shape and no state for "the check could not run". It is also the stage where the twelve fields do the most work: five of them are the anti-cheat set.

Why this is S9.1 and not S13

S9 is modelled in the corpus as a loop node with two entry edges — the review's finding set and parity's — and a proposal to split it into S9a/S9b was reduced to separate caps because renumbering breaks the spine, the anchors and every count (SUG-GATE-09). This example follows that decision: S9.1 is the verification half of S9, addressed as a stage because it has all twelve fields, and the thirteen-stage spine is unchanged.

The tag conflict, recorded rather than resolved

P-52 is ADOPTED and closes a finding only when the named check, the full suite and the holdouts pass. The holdouts are P-42 and the canaries P-43, both PROPOSED — and rules.md §1 says a PROPOSED rule may not block a merge. Under the directory's own semantics a finding whose only failing instrument is a holdout is unclosable. The stage's response is the only honest one available: the verdict records holdouts.regime as adopted or advisory, and F4 at UAT inherits the same field. Either the two rules are promoted or P-52's holdout clause is restated as conditional; that is a tag decision, not a stage decision (SUG-VER-10, pipeline.html §12 Q2).

The method, in order

  1. Resolve. Each finding's named check is looked up in the frozen manifest. An id that does not resolve, or that resolves to a skipped or empty test, is REFUSE-INPUT — the reproduce step otherwise trusts the fixer's naming (SUG-VER-12).
  2. Hash. Subject tree, manifest, test manifest, holdout set and environment recipe. A manifest path that is new is as much a breach as one that changed (SUG-VER-04).
  3. Run twice. The named check and the full suite, in an ephemeral environment, twice. Two runs are required before either PASS or FAIL; P-45 already asks this of integration tests (SUG-VER-07).
  4. Inject the holdouts. Stratified by rule id and module, injected at run time, never written to the repository. A failure reports the assertion id and a redacted diff, never the assertion text.
  5. Compute. The judge identity reads the runner's log and the manifest only, and computes one of four results. It executes nothing and writes nothing.
  6. Sign. The signer, which never executes branch code and holds the key outside the job, signs the record and appends it to the ledger with the previous entry's hash.

What this example leaves open, deliberately

Four things the corpus has not decided, and a stage cannot decide for it: who owns the holdout store and for how long (D10, Q1); which identity signs and where the key lives — a Jenkins credential, a KMS key or Sigstore keyless (Q4); what false-pass rate lets a verdict auto-merge, and who measures it on a corpus that does not exist (Q3); and whether the verifier runs the implementer's tests at all, or only the layer oracle (Q6). How to use the table

Score first, then read down the field column. A stage that fails C2 will match AP-02 and nothing else; a stage that fails C5 usually matches two of AP-05, AP-06 and AP-10 at once, because the three are one defect seen at three points in the timeline. The attribution matters for a second reason: the SUG ids are the evidence, so a reader who disagrees with a repair can go to the file and check whether the failure is real before arguing about the fix.

One anti-pattern this page adds, and does not number

Scoring your own stage. A rubric applied by the author of the thing being scored is a 16/16 that measures nothing — the same failure the corpus names for reviews and for the adjudicator. The control is the sampled audit: someone who did not write the stage reads the artifact in full, and a miss is a finding about the checks rather than about the sample (P-76, D9).

08How a stage cites the standards pack

A stage does not load "the standards". It names a domain slice, by id, and the worker loads only that slice.

Sources: docs/standards/README.md; docs/standards/registry.json; rules.md §1; SUG-ROLE-36 (rejected); pipeline.html §12 Q19

The pack is ninety rules in five domains, written to be loaded one domain at a time and cited by id. That sentence is the whole citation rule, and it is also the part the pipeline has not decided: SUG-ROLE-36 proposed attaching domains to every role card and was rejected, because the pack is not one of the nine documents and no pipeline rule assigns its ids to a role. What follows is therefore a convention, not an enforced mapping — and it is what a stage's binds field needs in order to name a rule at all.

R-*The retired draft's ids, folded into the pack64, R1–R64, all present and uniqueOnly through a rule's Provenance field

A citation is the id and nothing else. The registry entry is machine-readable — id, domain, title, status, severity, file, line, applies_to, provenance, summary — and the prose authority carries Why, How to check and Evidence. Where the two disagree, the domain file wins for the rule text and the registry is regenerated, which is why a citation never quotes the rule's text: it quotes the id.

The slicing rule

  • Name a domain, not the pack. "The standards pack" is not a value a stage can bind; it is a corpus a reader can browse. A stage names data-access and the ids inside it.
  • A domain per stage where possible. The pack's own boundary rule is that a rule lives in exactly one file; where a rule touches a second domain, the other file references its id rather than restating it. SEFL-ARCH-009 (typed contracts) and SEFL-SEC-005 (input validation) are the pair that shows the split.
  • Narrow inside the domain with applies_to. The values are web, service, batch, consumer, handler, library, dal, tests, review, all-java; a DAL stage loads the dal and all-java entries, not all sixteen data-access rules.
  • Say "none" when it is none. S0 and S1 bind no standards rule: a charter and a change log are not code, and inventing a subset for them is how a mapping becomes decoration.

The load, as a command

The registry is a single JSON file, so a worker's rule subset is a query, not a reading exercise. This is the DAL Reviewer's slice — data access plus security, narrowed to the mapping and the statement:

jq -r '.rules[]
       | select((.domain == "data-access" or .domain == "security")
                and (.applies_to | index("dal") or index("all-java")))
       | [.id, .severity, .title] | @tsv' docs/standards/registry.json

Twenty-six rules qualify at the domain level — 16 data-access and 10 security — and twenty-one survive the applies_to narrowing: the other five security rules apply to review, web or service rather than to the mappings. Both counts are in the counts table. Three of the 21 rows it returns:

SEFL-SEC-002	Blocker	Secrets never appear in source, committed configuration, logs or error bodies
SEFL-DATA-004	Blocker	Every statement is verified, and every data access endpoint has an integration test
SEFL-DATA-009	Blocker	Configuration tables are reached only through their procedures

That output is the whole justification for the citation convention: a stage that binds SEFL-DATA-004 has named a check a worker can run, and a stage that binds "data access standards" has named a shelf.

What this page is, and is not

It is a reading of eleven files at commit ae431e5, organised as a test a pipeline stage can be held to: twelve fields, eight criteria, five criteria for the agent, one definition in YAML, one prompt skeleton, one worked example and thirteen attributed failures.

It is not a new stage, a change to any of the thirteen, or a decision on any of the thirteen open decisions. Nothing in it runs. The thresholds (12/16, 8/10, three criteria locked at 2), the calibration scores, the per-stage standards slice and the S9.1 identifier are this page's proposals, marked † where they appear; the criteria, the failures and the citations are the documents'.

It deliberately does not renumber the spine, resolve the PROPOSED-rule conflict, name who signs G2, or decide where the holdout store lives. Those are the corpus's open questions, and a guide that answered them would be a design decision wearing a guide's clothes.
